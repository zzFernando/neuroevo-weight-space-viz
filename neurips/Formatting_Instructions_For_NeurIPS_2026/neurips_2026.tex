\begin{abstract}
Neuroevolutionary algorithms are increasingly applied to robotics and control,
yet their internal dynamics remain opaque: practitioners monitor only scalar
fitness curves, which cannot reveal whether a population is genuinely exploring
weight space or has silently converged to a sub-optimal mode.
We present \textbf{NeuroEvo-Viz}, an open-source interactive tool that makes
this dynamics visible by rendering the full population trajectory in a shared
2-D embedding at every generation.
The system contributes three technical components:
(i) \emph{temporally aligned UMAP}---a post-hoc cumulative-mean reference that
suppresses the arbitrary coordinate drift between independent per-generation
projections, making cross-generation comparisons meaningful without modifying
the UMAP objective;
(ii) a \emph{smoothed velocity field} that encodes the mean per-individual
displacement on a regular grid, rendered as streamlines to reveal convergence
basins; and
(iii) a \emph{per-cell divergence map}
($\nabla \cdot \mathbf{F} = \partial U / \partial x + \partial V / \partial y$)
that \emph{spatially separates} exploration from exploitation---the first such
measure we are aware of that operates at per-region resolution directly in
embedding space, rather than collapsing the population into a single scalar.
We demonstrate that these three components together answer questions that fitness
curves cannot: ``Has the population converged prematurely?'',
``Where in weight space is diversity being maintained?'', and
``When did exploration end?''
Case studies across four benchmarks---Make Moons, Scikit-learn Digits, a
NumPy proxy of the BRAX Ant locomotion task, and CIFAR-10---show qualitatively
distinct and interpretable dynamics in each domain.
All components run in pure Python (NumPy, SciPy, scikit-learn, Matplotlib,
Plotly, Streamlit) via a reproducible \texttt{pixi} environment, and the
codebase is released under the MIT licence.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Neuroevolution---the use of evolutionary algorithms to optimise neural network
weights, architectures, or both---has regained broad interest following
demonstrations of competitive performance on continuous control
\citep{salimans2017evolution,such2017deep} and open-ended exploration tasks
\citep{stanley2019designing}.
In principle, its population-based nature should make it more transparent than
gradient descent: we have access to every individual at every generation,
not just the endpoint.
In practice, this advantage is squandered---published experiments routinely
report only a single mean-fitness curve per run, discarding all spatial
information about how the search proceeds.

This opacity has concrete costs.
A flat fitness plateau looks identical whether it reflects a well-spread
population actively maintaining diversity, or a collapsed population with all
individuals within noise distance of the same local optimum.
In the former case, waiting longer is productive; in the latter, it is
not.
Without visualising the population geometry, the practitioner cannot tell
which regime they are in, leading to wasted compute and missed early-stopping
opportunities.

Dimensionality-reduction methods such as
PCA \citep{pearson1901pca},
$t$-SNE \citep{vandermaaten2008tsne}, and UMAP \citep{mcinnes2018umap}
can project high-dimensional weight vectors to two dimensions.
However, applying UMAP independently to each generation produces projections
that are \emph{not mutually aligned}: the rotation, scale, and reflection
change arbitrarily between calls, so apparent ``movement'' in the embedding
may reflect coordinate-system drift rather than actual population change.
No existing tool---to our knowledge---addresses this alignment problem for
the neuroevolution setting.

We present \textbf{NeuroEvo-Viz}, which closes this gap.
Our key claim is that \emph{the combination of temporally aligned UMAP,
a smoothed velocity field, and a per-cell divergence map together reveals
structure in neuroevolutionary search that scalar diagnostics cannot, and that
this structure is interpretable and reproducible across four qualitatively
different benchmarks.}

The tool currently supports the simple Gaussian ES;
we acknowledge that UMAP stochasticity and the absence of Procrustes-style
alignment mean that projections should be interpreted qualitatively and
verified across multiple seeds.
These limitations are detailed in Section~\ref{sec:limits}.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Temporally aligned UMAP} (Section~\ref{sec:align}).
        A lightweight $O(N)$ post-hoc alignment that anchors each generation's
        projection to a running cumulative-mean reference, enabling
        meaningful cross-generation comparison.

  \item \textbf{Population velocity field} (Section~\ref{sec:velfield}).
        Mean per-individual displacement binned on a regular grid and
        smoothed with a Gaussian kernel, rendered as streamlines (showing
        convergence flow) or quiver arrows (showing local magnitude).

  \item \textbf{Per-cell divergence map} (Section~\ref{sec:divergence}).
        $\nabla \cdot \mathbf{F}$ computed via central differences, with a
        symmetric TwoSlopeNorm colourmap and annotated zero-boundary contour
        that spatially delineates exploration ($\text{div}>0$) from
        exploitation ($\text{div}<0$) regions.

  \item \textbf{Multi-dataset interactive Streamlit application}
        (Section~\ref{sec:system}).
        Four benchmarks, bilingual UI, real-time parameter tuning, static
        and interactive render modes, all in a reproducible \texttt{pixi}
        environment.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Background}
\label{sec:background}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

\subsection{Neuroevolution setting}

Let $\theta \in \mathbb{R}^d$ denote the flattened weight vector of a neural
network.
At each generation $g \in \{0,\ldots,G-1\}$ the algorithm maintains a
population $\mathcal{P}_g = \{\theta^{(g)}_1, \ldots, \theta^{(g)}_N\}$ of
$N$ individuals.
A fitness function $f: \mathbb{R}^d \to \mathbb{R}$ assigns a scalar score
to each individual.
The simple Gaussian Evolution Strategy used throughout this paper selects
the top-$k$ elite individuals and reproduces them with additive Gaussian noise:
\begin{equation}
  \theta^{(g+1)}_i \;=\; \theta^{(g)}_{\pi(i)} \;+\; \varepsilon_i,
  \qquad \varepsilon_i \sim \mathcal{N}(0,\,\sigma^2 I),
  \label{eq:es}
\end{equation}
where $\pi(i)$ selects uniformly at random from the elites.
This strategy is deliberately simple so that the visualisations carry clear
ground truth: the population \emph{should} converge toward fitness peaks, and
the mutation noise $\sigma$ controls diversity in a predictable way.

\subsection{The UMAP alignment problem}

UMAP \citep{mcinnes2018umap} learns a low-dimensional embedding by preserving
the local neighbourhood structure of the data.
When applied independently to each generation $\mathcal{P}_g$, each call to
\texttt{fit\_transform} produces a projection
$\mathbf{P}_g \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times 2}$ defined only up to an arbitrary
rotation, reflection, and global translation.
Consequently, even if the population barely moves in weight space, the
embeddings of consecutive generations can appear to jump arbitrarily---a
visual artefact that would dominate any trajectory plot.
Our alignment procedure in Section~\ref{sec:align} addresses this directly.

\section{System Architecture}
\label{sec:system}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

\subsection{Overview}

NeuroEvo-Viz is structured as three independent layers:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Data layer} (\texttt{datasets.py}, \texttt{utils.py}).
        Implements four benchmark datasets---Make Moons, Scikit-learn Digits,
        a NumPy proxy of BRAX Ant, and CIFAR-10---behind a shared
        \texttt{NeuroEvoBase} interface.
        Each dataset exposes \texttt{evaluate(individual)} and
        \texttt{evolve\_one\_generation()}; the evolution loop in
        \texttt{utils.py} is dataset-agnostic.

  \item \textbf{Visualisation layer} (\texttt{visualizations/}).
        Five modules handle Aligned UMAP, Vector Field, Improved Vector Field,
        Trajectory Bundling, and shared colour-normalisation helpers.

  \item \textbf{Application layer} (\texttt{app.py}).
        A Streamlit application wires the two layers together, managing
        parameter state, \texttt{st.cache\_data} memoisation of expensive
        computations, and bilingual (English/Portuguese) UI strings.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Reproducible environment}

Dependencies are pinned in \texttt{pixi.toml} and locked in \texttt{pixi.lock},
guaranteeing bit-for-bit reproducibility across Linux, macOS (Intel and Apple
Silicon), and Windows.
The key constraint is \texttt{numpy~1.26.x} paired with \texttt{numba~0.60.x}
to avoid threading incompatibilities under Streamlit reruns; the environment
variable \texttt{NUMBA\_THREADING\_LAYER=omp} is set unconditionally at
startup.

\section{Visualisation Modules}
\label{sec:visualizations}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

\subsection{Temporally Aligned UMAP}
\label{sec:align}

The core insight is that we do not need a globally consistent UMAP---we only
need \emph{local coherence} between consecutive generations.
After fitting UMAP independently on generation $g$, we apply a post-hoc
translation toward a cumulative-mean reference:
\begin{equation}
  \hat{\mathbf{P}}_g \;=\; \mathbf{P}_g \;-\; \lambda_{\text{align}}
    \bigl(\mathbf{P}_g - \mathbf{P}^{\text{ref}}\bigr),
  \label{eq:align}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{P}^{\text{ref}} \;\leftarrow\;
    \frac{g \cdot \mathbf{P}^{\text{ref}} + \hat{\mathbf{P}}_g}{g+1},
  \label{eq:refupdate}
\end{equation}
where $\lambda_{\text{align}} \in [0,1]$ controls alignment strength.
Setting $\lambda_{\text{align}} = 0$ recovers the unaligned projections;
$\lambda_{\text{align}} = 1$ pulls every generation maximally toward the
reference centroid.
The cumulative-mean update in Equation~\eqref{eq:refupdate} ensures the
reference stabilises as the population converges, so late-generation
projections are pulled less aggressively than early ones.

This approach is lightweight---$O(N)$ per generation after the UMAP
fit---and does not require solving a Procrustes alignment problem or retraining
across generations.
Its key limitation is that it corrects only translation, not rotation or scale;
a full Procrustes solution would be more accurate but requires a bijective
correspondence between generations (see Section~\ref{sec:limits}).

\subsection{Population Velocity Field}
\label{sec:velfield}

Given the aligned embeddings $\{\hat{\mathbf{P}}_g\}$, the per-individual
velocity at generation $g$ is
$\mathbf{v}^{(g)}_i = \hat{\mathbf{P}}^{(g+1)}_i - \hat{\mathbf{P}}^{(g)}_i$.
To produce a field that is smooth and spatially interpretable, we bin these
velocities onto a regular $R \times R$ grid covering the full embedding bounding
box.
For cell $(r,c)$:
\begin{equation}
  U_{r,c} \;=\; \frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}_{r,c}|}
    \sum_{g,\,i \,\in\, \mathcal{I}_{r,c}} v^{(g)}_{i,x},
  \qquad
  V_{r,c} \;=\; \frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}_{r,c}|}
    \sum_{g,\,i \,\in\, \mathcal{I}_{r,c}} v^{(g)}_{i,y},
  \label{eq:velgrid}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{I}_{r,c}$ is the index set of all (generation, individual)
pairs whose source point $\hat{\mathbf{P}}^{(g)}_i$ falls in cell $(r,c)$.
The result is smoothed with a 2-D Gaussian kernel
(SciPy \texttt{gaussian\_filter}) before rendering.

\paragraph{Rendering modes.}
\emph{Streamlines} (Matplotlib \texttt{streamplot}) integrate the velocity
field forward to draw continuous flow lines, making convergence basins
visually salient.
\emph{Quiver} plots draw one arrow per grid cell and are better suited for
inspecting local magnitude differences.

\subsection{Divergence Analysis}
\label{sec:divergence}

The divergence of the velocity field,
\begin{equation}
  \nabla \cdot \mathbf{F} \;=\; \frac{\partial U}{\partial x}
    \;+\; \frac{\partial V}{\partial y},
  \label{eq:divergence}
\end{equation}
is computed via central differences (NumPy \texttt{gradient}, second-order
accuracy) and lightly smoothed to reduce finite-difference edge artefacts.
A symmetric \texttt{TwoSlopeNorm} colourmap (RdBu\_r) maps positive
divergence (red, \emph{exploration}) to negative divergence
(blue, \emph{exploitation}), with the zero boundary rendered as a solid
black contour.
Arrow annotations are placed automatically at the cells of maximal positive
and negative divergence, providing immediate spatial context without
requiring manual inspection.

This is, to our knowledge, the first spatially resolved exploration--exploitation
measure applied directly in weight-space embedding coordinates, complementing
existing scalar diversity metrics \citep{eiben2003introduction} that collapse
the population to a single number.

\subsection{Time-Weighted Kernel Density Estimate}
\label{sec:kde}

To highlight the final convergence basin, we compute a Gaussian KDE over all
embedded points with generation-proportional weights:
\begin{equation}
  w_i \;=\; 1 + 4 \cdot \frac{g(i)}{G-1},
  \label{eq:timeweight}
\end{equation}
so that individuals from the last generation receive $5\times$ the weight of
generation-zero individuals.
The KDE is evaluated on a $100 \times 100$ grid and rendered as a filled
contour plot (Magma colourmap, \texttt{PowerNorm} $\gamma = 0.5$) to enhance
structure at low densities.

\subsection{Adaptive Hexbin Scatter with Temporal Trail}

The first panel of the $2\!\times\!2$ layout replaces a raw scatter plot
(which overplots tens of thousands of points) with hexagonal binning reporting
the \emph{mean fitness} of all individuals in each bin.
The top $p$\% of individuals (default $p=5$) are overlaid as gold stars,
ensuring high-fitness loci are visible even at high population density.
The $k$ most recent generations (default $k=5$) are drawn as a fading crimson
trail, conveying temporal direction without animation.

\subsection{Adaptive Vector Subsampling}
\label{sec:subsample}

In dense regions of the embedding, grid-cell vectors overlap and create visual
noise.
We address this with a $k$-NN--based retention probability: the mean distance
from each valid grid cell to its $k$ nearest population points is computed;
cells in denser regions receive a lower retention probability (default
$p_{\text{dense}} = 0.30$), while sparse cells are retained almost entirely
(default $p_{\text{sparse}} = 0.90$).
This subsampling is used for the quiver fallback when \texttt{streamplot}
fails on degenerate fields.

Full BRAX \citep{freeman2021brax} requires JAX and a hardware accelerator.

MLP: $27 \to h \to 8$; default $h=64$, $d_w = 35 \times 64 = 2240$.

\paragraph{CIFAR-10.}
The 3\,072-dimensional input makes this the highest-dimensional benchmark.
MLP: $3072 \to h \to 10$ with softmax output; default $h=64$,
$d_w = 3082 \times 64 = 197\,248$.
We subsample 3\,000 training points for speed.

\paragraph{Exploration--exploitation transition.}
Panel A confirms that high-fitness individuals ($\bigstar$) cluster in a
compact region by generation 80, and the crimson trail shows the population
drifting steadily inward over the last five generations.
Panel B's streamlines confirm convergent flow toward this cluster, with the
attractor marker ($\bigstar$) placed at the velocity minimum.
Panel C shows the time-weighted KDE basin is well-separated from the
early-generation spread.
Panel D shows that by generation 80 the dominant colour is blue
(exploitation), with residual red only at the periphery where mutation still
occasionally expands the population---exactly the expected behaviour for a
converging ES.

\paragraph{Effect of alignment lambda.}
Reducing $\lambda_{\text{align}}$ from 0.8 to 0.0 increases the visible
cross-generation jump in Panel B's streamlines: without alignment, stream
origins and destinations drift erratically, obscuring the true flow direction.
Setting $\lambda_{\text{align}} = 1.0$ over-aligns, collapsing all generations
onto a single cluster.
The intermediate value of $0.8$ is empirically best for visualising gradual
convergence.

\paragraph{Locomotion vs.\ classification.}
The BRAX Ant proxy produces qualitatively different dynamics from Make Moons.
The velocity field is less radially symmetric, with multiple competing flow
directions reflecting the multi-modal nature of locomotion policies.
Divergence maps show extended exploration regions even at late generations,
consistent with the evolutionary search not having committed to a single gait
strategy---a diagnostic that a fitness curve alone would not reveal.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

\paragraph{Visualising neural network loss landscapes.}
\citet{li2018losslandscapes} introduced filter-normalised 2-D projections of
loss surfaces, demonstrating that skip connections correlate with flatter
landscapes.
\citet{goodfellow2015qualitative} used linear interpolations between trained
solutions to characterise saddle-point geometry.
These works project the \emph{landscape}; NeuroEvo-Viz projects the
\emph{population trajectory through that landscape}, which is a distinct and
complementary view.

\paragraph{Visualising evolutionary search.}
\citet{lehman2011novelty} used 2-D behaviour-space plots to illustrate
novelty-search dynamics.
Quality-Diversity (QD) algorithms
\citep{mouret2015illuminating,cully2015robots} routinely visualise archive
coverage in behaviour space.
These methods are specific to behaviour-space embeddings; they do not address
weight-space geometry or the alignment problem that arises when per-generation
UMAPs are not mutually consistent.

\paragraph{Dimensionality reduction for deep learning populations.}
\citet{fort2019deepens} applied PCA to track weight trajectories of a deep
ensemble, finding that ensembles occupy diverse loss-landscape modes.
Our work extends this idea to evolutionary populations across all generations
simultaneously, and adds the alignment layer needed to make per-generation
comparisons valid.

\paragraph{Temporal trajectory bundling.}
Trajectory bundling \citep{holten2009force} groups spatially nearby paths to
reduce visual clutter; it has been applied to eye-tracking and network
visualisation.
We implement a related bundling module (Catmull-Rom smoothing, beta-attraction)
for per-individual neuroevolution trajectories.

\paragraph{Exploration--exploitation analysis.}
The exploration--exploitation trade-off is central to evolutionary
computation \citep{eiben2003introduction}.
Scalar diversity metrics (e.g., average pairwise distance in weight space) are
standard but collapse all spatial information to a single number.
Our divergence map provides the first per-region spatial quantification of this
trade-off directly in the embedding, allowing practitioners to identify
\emph{which part} of the search space is being explored or exploited at any
generation.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limits}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

\paragraph{UMAP stochasticity.}
Even with a fixed \texttt{random\_state}, small changes in population content
can alter the UMAP topology substantially, because the approximate
nearest-neighbour graph is sensitive to density variations.
The alignment step reduces but does not eliminate this effect; users should
compare at least three independent seeds before drawing conclusions from any
single projection.

\paragraph{Translation-only alignment.}
Equation~\eqref{eq:align} corrects only the global translation of each
projection; it does not account for rotation or scale differences between
generations.
A full Procrustes alignment \citep{gower1975procrustes} would be more accurate
but requires a bijective correspondence between generation pairs of potentially
unequal size.
For smooth convergence trajectories, translation correction is sufficient; for
rapid structural transitions (e.g., sudden fitness jumps), the alignment may
introduce residual artefacts.

\paragraph{High-dimensional weight spaces.}
For CIFAR-10 ($d_w \approx 197\text{k}$), UMAP compresses an enormous amount
of structure into two dimensions.
The embeddings should be interpreted qualitatively; quantitative claims about
distances or cluster membership require further validation beyond what two
principal UMAP dimensions can support.

\paragraph{Simple ES only.}
The current implementation supports only the truncation-selection Gaussian ES
of Equation~\eqref{eq:es}.
More sophisticated algorithms (CMA-ES, OpenAI-ES, NEAT) produce weight-space
dynamics driven by adaptation operators (covariance matrix updates,
perturbation-based gradients, topological mutations) that the tool has not been
validated on, and whose dynamics may not decompose cleanly into the
exploration/exploitation framing assumed here.

\paragraph{BRAX proxy fidelity.}
The NumPy BRAX proxy captures the correct dimensionality and qualitative
dynamics but not the contact physics of the original MuJoCo simulation.
Conclusions drawn from the proxy should be re-validated on the full BRAX
environment before being treated as ground truth for locomotion policies.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

We presented NeuroEvo-Viz, an open-source interactive tool for visualising the
weight-space dynamics of neuroevolutionary search.
The three core technical contributions---temporally aligned UMAP, population
velocity fields, and per-cell divergence maps---together enable practitioners
to diagnose three failure modes that scalar fitness curves cannot detect:
premature convergence to a sub-optimal basin, loss of population diversity
before the fitness plateau, and spatially heterogeneous exploration/exploitation
in multi-modal landscapes.

These diagnostics matter beyond academic interest.
As neuroevolution is increasingly applied to safety-critical domains (robotic
locomotion, autonomous systems), the ability to inspect \emph{how} a policy
population evolves---not just the final fitness score---is a prerequisite for
building justified confidence in the resulting controllers.
A practitioner who can observe that their population collapsed to a single mode
in generation 15 can rerun with higher mutation noise; without this visibility,
they cannot.

The publication-ready $2\!\times\!2$ layout demonstrated across four benchmarks
provides a template for reporting neuroevolution experiments with richer spatial
context.
We hope it raises the standard for empirical transparency in the field.

Future work includes supporting modern ES variants (CMA-ES, OpenAI-ES),
Procrustes-based alignment for stronger temporal coherence, and integrating
trajectory bundling as a fifth panel to reveal per-individual paths rather than
aggregate flows.

\section{Implementation Details}
\label{app:impl}

\subsection{Alignment algorithm}

The temporally aligned UMAP procedure is given below.
The alignment is applied after each independent UMAP \texttt{fit\_transform}
call and requires no modification to the UMAP library.

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{l}
\toprule
\textbf{Algorithm 1}: Temporally aligned UMAP \\
\midrule
\textbf{Input}: $\{\mathcal{P}_g\}_{g=0}^{G-1}$, $\lambda \in [0,1]$,
               UMAP hyperparameters \\
\textbf{Output}: Aligned embeddings $\{\hat{\mathbf{P}}_g\}$ \\
\midrule
$\mathbf{P}^{\text{ref}} \leftarrow \texttt{None}$ \\
\textbf{for} $g = 0$ \textbf{to} $G-1$ \textbf{do} \\
\quad $\mathbf{P}_g \leftarrow \texttt{UMAP.fit\_transform}(\mathcal{P}_g)$ \\
\quad \textbf{if} $\mathbf{P}^{\text{ref}} = \texttt{None}$ \textbf{then}
      $\hat{\mathbf{P}}_g \leftarrow \mathbf{P}_g$;
      $\mathbf{P}^{\text{ref}} \leftarrow \mathbf{P}_g$ \\
\quad \textbf{else} \\
\quad\quad $\hat{\mathbf{P}}_g \leftarrow
      \mathbf{P}_g - \lambda(\mathbf{P}_g - \mathbf{P}^{\text{ref}})$ \\
\quad\quad $\mathbf{P}^{\text{ref}} \leftarrow
      (g \cdot \mathbf{P}^{\text{ref}} + \hat{\mathbf{P}}_g) / (g+1)$ \\
\quad \textbf{end if} \\
\textbf{end for} \\
\textbf{return} $\{\hat{\mathbf{P}}_g\}$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\subsection{Velocity grid}

The velocity grid in Equation~\eqref{eq:velgrid} uses
$\min_g |\mathcal{P}_g|$ individuals per generation to handle variable
population sizes.
Cells with fewer than \texttt{min\_vectors\_per\_cell} observations are masked
and excluded from rendering.
The default grid resolution is $R = 30$.

\subsection{Benchmark details}

\paragraph{Make Moons.}
Generated with \texttt{make\_moons(n\_samples=1000, noise=0.25)}.
MLP: $2 \to h \to 1$ with $\tanh$ hidden activations and sigmoid output;
no bias terms, so $d_w = 2h + h = 3h$.

\paragraph{Scikit-learn Digits.}
Full 1\,797-sample split; no train/test separation (fitness is
in-distribution, as is conventional in evolutionary robotics).
MLP: $64 \to h \to 1$; $d_w = 64h + h = 65h$.

\paragraph{BRAX Ant proxy.}
8 joints, 27-D observation (position $x$, velocity $\dot{x}$, joint angles,
joint velocities, orientation stub).
Episode: 100 steps, $\Delta t = 0.05$\,s.
Forward dynamics: $\ddot{x} = 0.3\langle\mathbf{a},\cos\boldsymbol{\theta}\rangle
- 0.1\dot{x}$, where $\mathbf{a}$ is the 8-D action and $\boldsymbol{\theta}$
the joint-angle vector.
Fitness: $\sum_t \max(0, \dot{x}_t) - 0.001\|\mathbf{a}_t\|^2$.
MLP: $27 \to h \to 8$; $d_w = 27h + 8h = 35h$.

\paragraph{CIFAR-10.}
Downloaded from \url{https://www.cs.toronto.edu/~kriz/cifar-10-python.tar.gz}
and cached in \texttt{\textasciitilde/.cache/neuroevo/}.
3\,000 samples drawn uniformly at random.
MLP: $3072 \to h \to 10$ with softmax output; $d_w = 3072h + 10h = 3082h$.
